Section Exercise Budgets

A proposal to change how many practice exercises each section of a book gets. Today a fixed formula sets the number from the objective count. Under this proposal, Python works out a sensible range for each section from the textbook's own questions and its objectives, and the model decides where in that range the section belongs.

Proposal for discussion Revised 18 November 2026 Nothing implemented yet

In short

  • The model judges, Python does the arithmetic. The model picks a level from 1 to 5 for the section and for each objective. Python turns those levels into exercise counts.
  • Every section has a range. At least 20 exercises and at most 148, and between 1.5 and 3 times what the textbook prints when it prints enough to go on.
  • Everything is a multiple of 4. Every total and every objective's share, so each objective's sets fill whole rows.
  • Same cost as today, or less. At most one model call per section, and none when no answer could change the result. Each budget is written once and only asked for again when its inputs change.

Why change the formula

Today every section gets 48 + 12 × objectives exercises, and the model only decides how to divide them between the objectives.

  • The objective count depends on how the spec was written. The same lesson written as 8 objectives gets 144 exercises. Written as 2 objectives, it gets 72.
  • Subject and grade play no part in the total. A Grade 3 Arabic section and a Grade 12 math section with the same objective count get the same number.
  • The textbook plays no part either. A section the textbook drills with 60 questions and one it barely touches are sized the same.
  • The floor is too high for small sections. Even one objective gets 60, which is a lot for a language section where one item can be a whole passage.

How the new budget works

  1. Extract the textbook's questions first. The source_questions stage moves ahead of budgets, so each section's question count is known before it is budgeted. A section being budgeted without a usable extract is refused.
  2. Python works out the section's range and five level totals. Both are explained in the next two parts.
  3. The model reads the section and picks levels. One level for the section, which picks its total, and one level per objective, which says how the total is shared. When no answer could change the result, Python skips the call and writes the budget itself.
  4. Python works out each objective's share in blocks of 4. Every objective gets one block first, and the rest of the total is shared in proportion to the objective levels.
  5. Python checks the reply and writes the budget once. A reply that fails a check is asked for again, up to 4 attempts as today. Later runs skip the section unless its spec or its extract changes, or the run is forced with --update.

The range for each section

Every section gets a least and a most number of exercises. Python works both out from how many questions the section's source books print and how many objectives it has.

s = questions the section's source books print ÷ number of source books
    (the average per book; for a one-book section, simply the count)
n = objectives in the section

minimum = max(1.5 × s, 4 × n, 20)    rounded up to a multiple of 4
maximum = max(3 × s, 20 × n)         rounded up to a multiple of 4
both capped at 148

The settings

SettingValueWhy
Least per objective4One row of a set. Enough for a definition or a piece of notation.
Most per objective20When the objectives set the range, the levels become 4, 8, 12, 16 and 20 per objective.
Section minimum20No section is too small for a real block of practice.
Textbook factors1.5 and 3Level 3 is about 2.25 times the textbook. A typical math section lands close to today's total.
Section cap148Just above today's largest common total (144, for 8 objectives).
Levels5Fine enough to tell sections apart, coarse enough that the model repeats its choice.

When the textbook prints no questions for a section, the objective count and the floor of 20 set the range, and the model is told there are none. When the count looks unreliable (under 2 questions per source page), it still counts towards the range, since the textbook prints at least that many. The model is told the count is low for its pages, which is normal for long items such as passages but may also mean some questions were missed.

Sections drawn from more than one book. Some Egyptian books draw every section from more than one source book. The extracted count is the total across all of them, so s is that total divided by the number of books, which is the average per book. Two books with 30 and 50 questions give s = 80 ÷ 2 = 40, so the section stays on the same scale as a one-book section. Every section of such a book has the same books and no question is printed in both, so the average is exact. Python warns if a book's sections ever have different numbers of books.

The five levels

Python then turns the range into five fixed totals, one per level. The model never sees this formula; it only picks one of the five totals.

level k = minimum + (k − 1) × (maximum − minimum) ÷ 4
          rounded to a multiple of 4, for k = 1 … 5

Reading the formula

The level formula places five evenly spaced stops between the section's minimum and maximum. Think of a ladder with five rungs: the bottom rung is the minimum and the top rung is the maximum.

PartWhat it means
minimum, maximumThe two ends of this section's range, from the previous part.
maximum − minimumThe distance from one end of the range to the other.
÷ 4Five levels have four gaps between them, so this is the size of one step.
(k − 1)How many steps to climb from level 1. Level 1 climbs none and stays at the minimum. Level 5 climbs four and reaches the maximum.
rounded to a multiple of 4Divide by 4, round to the nearest whole number (an exact half rounds up), then multiply by 4 again. Every level then fills whole rows of 4.

Worked example. A section with a minimum of 60 and a maximum of 120. The distance is 120 − 60 = 60, so one step is 60 ÷ 4 = 15.

Drawn to scale. The faint marks are the exact quarter points before rounding (75, 90 and 105). Rounding moves each level to its nearest multiple of 4, so the gaps come out as 16, 16, 12 and 16 instead of exactly 15 each.

  • When the steps are already multiples of 4, nothing moves. A section with no textbook questions and 5 objectives has a range of 20–100, so each step is 20 and the levels are 20, 40, 60, 80 and 100.
  • When the minimum equals the maximum, every level is the same. One objective with 5 textbook questions has a range of 20–20, so each step is 0 and all five levels are 20.

How the model decides the levels

In one call the model makes two kinds of judgment: one level for the section, which picks its total, and one level for each objective, which says how that total is shared. It never writes a number of exercises. Each level comes with a description, so the model is matching the section to a description rather than inventing a count.

Why levels instead of a number

  • A free number drifts. Asked twice, a model choosing any number between 20 and 100 can answer 40 and then 70. A choice from five fixed totals repeats, and when it does change, it moves a whole level, which is easy to spot.
  • The model still makes every judgment. Subject, grade, how demanding the content is, and how heavily the textbook drills the section compared with the rest of the book. Python only does the arithmetic.
  • Every reply can be checked exactly. With a free number, the only possible check is whether it falls inside the range.

The section level

How much practice the section needs, from light to heavy. Level 3 is the starting point, a typical lesson for this book, and the model moves away from it only for a reason it states in its reasoning.

LevelThe section looks likeExample in a Grade 7 math book
1Much lighter than a typical section: one or two simple objectives, mostly recognition, little that is new for this grade.A unit opener that introduces vocabulary
2A little lighter than typical: practice of skills learners already partly know.Reviewing place value before integers
3A typical lesson for this book and grade.Adding integers
4Heavier than typical: a new procedure, or several objectives that each need real practice.Multiplying and dividing integers
5Much heavier: a core procedure that needs fluency, multi-step reasoning, or common errors to unlearn.Solving two-step equations

What the model weighs to choose it:

  • How demanding the content is for this grade, read from the spec's teaching points, notation and page excerpts.
  • The objectives: how many there are, and how many of them are heavy.
  • The section's role: the spec says whether the section teaches, revises or assesses. Revision and assessment sections usually need less new practice, unless the spec shows they drill a lot.
  • Subject and grade: what one exercise asks of a learner, and how much repetition learners of this age need.
  • The book profile, to see what a typical section of this book looks like. If some sections have not been extracted yet, the profile says how many it covers.

Heavier means more demanding, not more questions. The range already grows with the textbook's question count, so a section the textbook drills more than most is not, on its own, a reason for a higher level. When the textbook prints no questions for the section, the model judges from the spec and the book profile alone.

The objective levels

How much of the section's practice each objective needs compared with the section's other objectives. These levels act as weights: every objective gets one block of 4 first, and Python shares the rest of the total in proportion to the levels.

LevelThe objective isExample
1Recognition: a definition, a piece of notation, a fact to state.Read and write integer notation
2A simple skill that needs only a little practice.Name the opposite of an integer
3A typical skill of the section.Place integers on a number line
4A demanding skill, or one the rest of the section builds on.Use integers in real contexts
5The section's core: a procedure that needs fluency.Compare and order integers
  • The model weighs breadth (how much of the section the objective takes up), depth of skill, cognitive demand (applying and justifying need more than identifying), how much later work depends on it, and where the spec warns of common errors.
  • The textbook's count per objective is a hint, not the answer. An objective the textbook never drills still needs real practice, so it is never given level 1 for that reason alone.
  • Giving every objective the same level is treated as not having decided. A section with one objective gives it level 5. Python writes 5 even if the model says otherwise, since that objective takes the whole total at any level, so a reply is never rejected for it.

Sharing the total between objectives

The section level picks the total. Python then shares it between the objectives in blocks of 4, using the objective levels as weights.

  1. Cut the total into blocks of 4.
  2. Give every objective one block first, so no objective ends below 4 exercises.
  3. Share the remaining blocks in proportion to the objective levels, rounding each share down.
  4. Give the blocks left over to the largest fractions, with ties going to the earlier objective in the spec.

Worked example. Level 3 of a 60–120 section is 92 exercises, which is 23 blocks. One block each leaves 23 − 4 = 19. The objective levels 1, 5, 3 and 3 add up to 12, so each objective's exact share of the 19 is 19 × its level ÷ 12. Rounding down uses 16 blocks and leaves 3, which go to the largest fractions (0.92, 0.75 and 0.75).

  • The shares always add up to the total, and each is at least 4 and a multiple of 4.
  • A higher level never gets fewer exercises than a lower one, and two equal levels end at most one block apart.
  • When the total is exactly 4 per objective, there is nothing left to share and the levels change nothing: 20 exercises for 5 objectives gives 4 each.
  • A section with one objective gives it the whole total.

3Python builds part 1 of the call: the instructions

The instructions are the stage's prompt file with two pieces filled in for this book. The highlighted parts are filled in by Python: the book context line from step 1, and the language rules for an English book (prompts/budgets/language/en.md). Every section of the book gets the same instructions.

You are an expert K–12 assessment designer deciding how much practice one section of a practice book needs, and how that practice is shared among the objectives the section teaches.

## Task
You receive one section: its reviewed spec (what it teaches, the objectives it serves, the teaching points behind them, its glossary, the equations and notations it uses, and short excerpts from the pages), how many questions the textbook itself prints for it, and a profile of the whole book. You make two judgements:

1. A section level from 1 to 5: how much practice this section needs. Each level stands for a fixed number of exercises, listed with the section.
2. An objective level from 1 to 5 for every objective: how much of the section's practice that objective needs compared with the section's other objectives.

You return levels only. The exercise counts are worked out from them.

Book context:
Subject: Mathematics | Grade: 7 | Language: English | Language code: en | Numerals: western | Notation: latin | Country: Republic of India

## You judge, you write nothing
Write no exercises. No stems, no questions, no worked examples, no sample items, not even one to illustrate a point. Another call writes the exercises later, from the counts your levels produce. Your entire answer is a level for the section, a level per objective, and a few sentences on how you arrived at them.

## The section level
The section comes with five totals, one per level, from the least to the most practice that suits it. They are already set from the textbook's question count and the number of objectives: do not recompute them, and do not answer with a number of your own. Choose the level from:

- Difficulty. How demanding the section's content is for this grade: new procedures, multi-step reasoning and subtle distinctions need more practice than definitions and recognition.
- The objectives. How many there are, and how many of them are heavy.
- The section's role. The spec's `role` says whether the section teaches, revises or assesses. Revision and assessment sections usually need less new practice than teaching sections, unless the spec shows they drill a lot.
- Subject and grade. What one exercise asks of a learner differs by subject, and what counts as enough practice differs by grade: younger learners need more repetition on each new skill.
- The book profile. Use it to see what a typical section of this book looks like. When the profile covers only some of the book's sections, compare against those.

A heavier section is a more demanding one, not one with more questions. The five totals already grow with the textbook's question count, so a section the textbook drills more than most is not, on its own, a reason to choose a higher level.

Level 3 is a typical section of this book. Levels 1 and 5 are for sections clearly lighter or clearly heavier than most. Say why you chose the level in `reasoning`.

When a section is drawn from more than one source book, its question count is also given as the average per book, and the book profile is on that same per-book scale.

When the section says its question count is low for its pages, that is normal for long items such as passages, but the extraction may also have missed questions: weigh the count together with the spec. When no questions were extracted, judge from the spec and the book profile alone.

## The objective levels
Each objective's level says how much of the section's practice it needs compared with the other objectives:

- 1: recognition, such as a definition, a piece of notation or a fact to state.
- 3: a typical skill of the section.
- 5: the section's core, such as a procedure that needs fluency or the skill the rest of the section depends on.

Weigh breadth, depth of skill, cognitive demand, foundation value and failure risk.

The textbook's count per objective is a hint, not the answer: one question can carry two objectives, questions tagged with none count toward the section but toward no objective, and an objective the textbook never drills still needs real practice. Never give an objective level 1 only because the textbook has no questions for it.

Giving every objective the same level is almost always a sign of not having decided. A section with one objective gives it level 5.

## Field rules
- level: the section level, a whole number from 1 to 5.
- objectives: one entry per objective in the spec, in the spec's own order, each with its `id` exactly as the spec prints it and its `level` from 1 to 5. Never invent, omit or merge an id.
- reasoning: two to four sentences in English, for the maintainer: first the section level and why, set against the spec and the book profile; then which objectives got the highest and lowest levels, and why. Name objectives by id.

## Language: English
The spec you are weighing is in English. Your `reasoning` is in English as well, as the field rule says, and names objectives by their ids rather than by quoting them.

## Abort
Abort only when the section cannot be judged at all: no objectives, or a spec so empty there is nothing to judge. On abort, give one line in `abort_reason` and return no level and no objectives.

## Output
Return the response envelope JSON: status, abort_reason, reasoning, level, objectives.

7Python checks the reply and works out the counts

Checks. A reply that fails any of them is asked for again, as in step 5.

  • The status is ok or abort. On abort, the section is reported in the run's summary and nothing is written.
  • The section level is a whole number from 1 to 5.
  • Every objective in the spec appears exactly once, and no id appears that the spec does not have.
  • Every objective level is a whole number from 1 to 5.

Two things are corrected rather than rejected. The objective levels are put back in the spec's order before sharing, so a tie always goes to the earlier objective in the spec, whatever order the model used. And a one-objective section is written as level 5.

Risks and how they are handled

RiskHow it is handled
The model puts most sections at the same levelRead the levels and the reasoning on the first book. If they bunch, adjust the level descriptions in the prompt or the factors.
Re-runs give different totalsBudgets are written once and skipped afterwards. A level choice repeats far more reliably than a free number.
Existing books are budgeted againOld budgets do not match the new level totals, so re-running an existing book budgets every section again, and those sections' exercises must then be written again. Python prints a warning for each. New books are not affected.
Translated books keep old budgetsA --translate run treats any existing budget file as done, so twin books would never pick up the new rules. Fix: count a budget file as done only when it is in the new format. The Arabic copy of each budget is also re-copied when the English one changes.
Small sections have little roomOne objective with 6 or fewer textbook questions always gets 20. At the floor, objective levels cannot fully separate: 20 exercises shared by levels 1, 5 and 3 gives 4, 8 and 8.
Heavy sections reach the capA section with 97 or more textbook questions gets 148 at every level, so its level makes no difference.
Spare exercises grow as a sharenew_questions writes 8 spare exercises per objective, so a share of 4 would mean 12 written for 4 printed. In this change, non-Arabic books write 4 spares for a share of 8 or less and 8 otherwise. Math books reject far fewer exercises than they have spares (225 rejected against 2,784 spares in one book). Arabic books keep 8 until their rejection rate is measured. The cap ships only after a before-and-after check of paper fill on one math book.
CostUnchanged or lower: at most one Flash call per section, none when no answer could change the result. The book profile adds about 1k tokens per call for the books checked.
